# Lane 13: time evidence for registration records, primary-evidence review

Prepared 2026-11-23. This review supports the [registration record design](../drafts/registration-record.md). It asks one question: which mechanisms can let a creator later show "this exact content existed, under my key, no later than time T", and at what cost. It is not a decision, a schema change or an implementation. Nothing was submitted to any time-stamping authority, log, calendar or witness. No package was installed and no account was created. Specifications and documentation were read over read-only HTTP at the versions in the [ledger](#source-ledger). Repository context: HEAD `0879905` on `roadmap-v2`. Read first: the [roadmap](../../ROADMAP.md) finding of 2026-11-23 (a mark is a finding aid, not evidence of origin), M4; [attestations](../../spec/attestations.md); [core](../../spec/core.md) (commitment, claimed times); [use cases](../../docs/use-cases.md) UC-3 and UC-4; the [signing interop results](signing-interop-results.md); the [C2PA mapping draft](../drafts/c2pa-mapping.md). Only this file belongs to this lane.

**Not legal advice.** The legal rows quote what two primary texts say. They do not say what any court would decide in any case, and nothing here should be relied on as legal advice.

## Decision

**Recommendation (needs the owner before any external service is used):** implement two time-evidence types first. Use **RFC 3161 time-stamp tokens** (T1), allowing tokens from more than one TSA, and **OpenTimestamps** Bitcoin attestations (T4). Their trust roots are unrelated: a TSA's certificate chain for T1, Bitcoin proof of work for T4. Each is verifiable offline once complete, sends only a digest, and needs no account. Each also covers the other's main weakness. T1 is precise to about a second and is the form that eIDAS (L1), C2PA (T6) and Sigstore (T3) already consume, but one TSA key compromise can backdate. T4 has no single operator that can backdate, but it is coarse (hours) and stays pending until a calendar's Bitcoin commitment confirms. Transparency logs (T3) and witness cosigning (T5) add public discoverability and split-view protection. They do not add a better time. Rekor v2 delegates time to a TSA, and a public log publishes the signer's key or identity for ever. They stay optional and owner-gated.

**Strongest alternative:** RFC 3161 only, from two TSAs run by unrelated operators. RFC 3161 itself names "two time-stamp tokens from two different TSAs" as a defence against a compromised TSA key (T1 §4 item 2). It needs one parser and no Bitcoin header source. It loses T4's operator-free root. **Confidence:** high for what each mechanism proves and discloses (normative text). Low for operational facts that the sources state without evidence: uptime, how long free services remain free, how long calendar data is retained.

## What every mechanism here has in common

Each mechanism time-stamps a **digest**. None of them sees content. So each proves at most that *someone* knew that digest by T. That implies the preimage existed by T, assuming SHA-256 preimage resistance (I). None proves that the registrant held the content, created it, or was first. Showing possession needs a value that only a content holder could have computed before T. The design's salted possession digest does this ([design](../drafts/registration-record.md) §4.3). The record cannot exclude a copier who also holds the content. **First to register is not first to create.**

## Findings that change the picture

1. **A transparency log is not a clock.** Rekor v1's time is unauthenticated by the log structure, and Rekor v2 has no time of its own (T3). A registration that cites a Rekor entry still needs T1 or T4 for its time. A log is useful for discoverability and monitoring of a key or identity. It has nothing to discover when registrations are private.
2. **Countersign the signature, not the content.** Sigstore recommends "signing over a signature … ensuring that the signature, not the artifact, was created at a certain time" (cosign timestamps). C2PA v2 time-stamps the claim signature (T6). For a registration this gives two properties. Stamping the DSSE signature proves the **signed statement** existed by T. A later key compromise therefore cannot produce new registrations that appear earlier (T1 §1, the revocation use). And the digest sent to services reveals nothing about the content (I; the statement carries a fresh nonce).
3. **Two roots are needed against backdating, not just against downtime.** If the verifier takes the earliest valid time, one dishonest or compromised root can backdate. Requiring agreement from q ≥ 2 independent roots does not have this weakness (I; RFC 3161 §4 item 2 gives the same advice for TSAs). The design reports both bounds.
4. **OpenTimestamps has a pending window.** Until `ots upgrade` succeeds, the proof depends on a calendar staying up and keeping its data (T4). The client submits to several calendars by default (README example: three). A registration tool must upgrade and re-save before the proof counts as evidence, and must report "pending" rather than "valid" before then.
5. **Privacy is decided by what is published, not by what is stamped.** TSAs and calendars see a digest and an IP address. A public log adds key or identity and time, for ever. Publishing the statement itself reveals the artifact digest, which confirms any guessed whole file. These are separable choices; the design keeps them separate.
6. **Longevity needs renewal.** T1 tokens expire with TSA keys and algorithms unless renewed (T1 §4 item 3; T9). T4 survives as long as SHA-256 and the Bitcoin header chain do. Neither lasts automatically "for decades".
